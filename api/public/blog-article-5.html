wallet screening vs. kyc: why you need both

chibbyaugust 9, 2026 · 5 min read

i used to think kyc was enough. i had a customer file to prove it: passport, proof of address, sanctions check, all green. six months later that same customer took money from a wallet tied to a sanctioned entity, and not one page in that file had a chance of catching it.

that is the part that stayed with me. kyc did not fail. kyc did exactly what it is built to do. it checked a person. nobody ever asked it to check the money.

you verify an identity. you look at an id. you run the name against a sanctions list. they pass, you onboard them, you move on. for a bank that is enough, because in a bank the money arrives from another regulated institution with a name attached to it.

crypto does not work like that. the money arrives from an address. the address has a history. and that history is the only thing in the whole transaction that has ever met a criminal.

one checks the person, the other checks the money

kyc happens once, at onboarding. name, address, government id. does this person exist, and is this person on a list. then you file it and you are done.

wallet screening happens every time money moves. you take the address and put it against blockchain risk data. has it ever touched a sanctioned entity. has it taken funds out of a mixer. does it sit in a cluster that leads to a darknet market. you get a score and a reason.

different questions, different moments, different threats. kyc tells you who somebody claims to be. screening tells you what the address has already done, whoever is holding it today.

which is why a customer can be completely real and completely clean on paper, and still hand you money that is not.

what it looks like when the gap opens

these are not hypotheticals. they are the two shapes i keep running into, written as they feel from your side of the desk.

the customer who passed everything

real passport. real address. biometric check, clean. the sanctions screen on the name comes back empty. you onboard them the same day and you are right to.

months later they receive funds from an address that traces back to a sanctioned entity. nothing about the person changed. nothing in your file was wrong. the risk was never in the person, it was in the address, and you never looked at the address because nobody told you it was part of the job.

the withdrawal nobody screened

same customer, three months on, withdraws to a wallet that has been fed by a mixer. that mixer is one a north korean group runs money through.

the regulator flags the transaction. your bank starts asking what you knew. and the honest answer is the worst one available: you have no record of ever checking that address, because you never checked any of them. kyc did not fail here either. it just never covered this.

what the rules actually say, and what they expect

this is where most people get it wrong in both directions, so it is worth being precise.

the transfer of funds regulation, the travel rule, makes casps send verified originator and beneficiary information with every transfer. for a self-hosted wallet above €1,000 you have to verify that your customer owns or controls the destination address. read that again: it is an identity question. it proves whose wallet it is. it says nothing about what that wallet has done.

so no, the travel rule does not order you to screen against sanctions lists, darknet records or scam databases. anyone telling you it does has not read it.

now the other direction. fatf expects screening as part of any serious crypto compliance program, and says plainly that vasps should apply sanctions screening and assess the risk attached to virtual assets and wallet addresses. mica does not name wallet screening either, but it does require casps to run systems that detect and prevent money laundering and market abuse, and screening is how that requirement gets met in practice. ofac has sanctioned entire protocols and individual addresses, which only means something if you are checking addresses against their lists.

the uk's joint money laundering steering group is the least ambiguous of the lot: casps should use blockchain analytics for ongoing monitoring of wallet addresses, as part of a risk-based approach. not in the travel rule text. very much in the guidance.

the gap lives between the text and the expectation

the law says verify ownership. the expectation is that you also know whether the address has been anywhere near a mixer, a darknet cluster or a laundering chain that crosses four networks.

that gap is not an oversight you can argue your way out of. it is where enforcement happens. the standard did not come from a statute, it came from the industry moving and regulators making clear what they now consider normal. by the time someone is asking you about it, "the regulation did not explicitly require it" is not a defence, it is a transcript.

the api call is the easy part

screening one wallet is not technically hard. you send an address, you get back a score, a level and a reason. it takes a fraction of a second and the code is a few lines.

the hard part is everything behind that answer. the data you need to be right is not in one place, it is scattered:

  • sanctions lists
  • scam databases
  • usdt blacklists
  • darknet records
  • cross-chain activity

you need all of them. you need to know which ones move daily and which sit still for months. you need to know which flag is a reason to stop and which is noise, because treating them the same is how you either block good customers or wave through bad ones.

and when you do flag a wallet, you need a reason you can put in front of your bank and defend out loud. that is the actual work. not the call.

bottom line

kyc tells you who your customer is. screening tells you what the money has been doing while nobody was looking. you need both. one without the other is a gap, and a gap is precisely what gets found.
