Why criminals target small businesses (and exactly how they do it)

ChibbyJuly 7, 2026 · 5 min read

"we're too small to be worth targeting." I believed that too, right up until I started reading the wallet histories of businesses exactly like yours. The dirty money doesn't skip the small guys. It looks for them.

Here is the part nobody tells you. A criminal moving illicit funds is not looking for the richest target. They are looking for the softest one. And softness has a very specific definition: can this business see where my money came from, or not.

A large exchange can. It has an AML team, live screening on every deposit, analysts whose entire job is to pull the thread on a suspicious wallet. Moving money through them is slow and risky, and it usually gets caught.

You are a different story. No screening tool watching your incoming payments. No analyst. Just you, trying to close deals and keep customers happy. To someone laundering money, that is not a small business. That is an open door with the lights off.

You're not collateral damage, you're the plan

This isn't a hunch. The firms that trace this for a living keep landing on the same conclusion. TRM labs has flagged that laundering techniques once reserved for banks are now being pointed at small operators, precisely because they are easier. Chainalysis says it flatly: smaller businesses lack the infrastructure to detect laundering, and that gap is exactly what makes them useful for pushing illicit funds back into the real economy.

Useful. Sit with that word. You are not accidentally caught up in someone else's scheme. In the scheme, you are the wash cycle.

And it works because everything that makes you good at running a business makes you blind to this:

  • You don't screen incoming wallets, because until now there was no simple way to
  • You can't investigate every payment, because you have a business to run
  • You trust paying customers, because 99% of the time trusting them is correct
  • You're not on a regulator's radar, so nobody is watching your back either
  • You move fast to win the deal, so you rarely stop to ask where the money has been

Every one of those is a strength on a normal Tuesday. Put a laundering operation on the other side of the table and every one of them becomes the way in.

Three ways it actually plays out

These are not scare stories. They are the shapes that show up over and over in Chainalysis and Elliptic case work, rewritten as what they feel like from your side of the desk.

The clean new client

A new customer wants your service and pays 10,000 USDT upfront, no haggling. They have a real website, a real linkedin, they even take a video call. Everything about them checks out, so you deliver and move on.

Three months later Tether freezes that 10,000 USDT on-chain. The address it came from traces back to a sanctioned entity. You had no way of knowing, and it changes nothing: the funds are gone, your wallet is flagged, and you are the one writing the explanation. The wallet always knew. You just couldn't see it.

The loyal client, new address every time

This one pays like clockwork. Every month, never late, the kind of customer you wish you had ten of. The only quirk is a fresh wallet address on each payment. "security, we rotate wallets," they tell you, and honestly that sounds responsible.

What you can't see from the address alone is that all of them fan out from the same cluster, and that cluster is tied to a darknet market. For six months you have been the clean, legitimate-looking exit point for a criminal operation, and it was invisible because you were only ever looking at one address at a time.

The urgent one

A client calls in a hurry. They need it now, they send the money immediately, they ask you to ship today. You are a good operator who delivers under pressure, so you do.

That payment traces to a ransomware payout. A hospital, a school, some business somewhere paid to get their files unlocked, and the funds moved straight to you. You didn't commit the crime. But on-chain, you are now the next hop in an extortion trail, and that trail has your name on it.

Having a policy is not the same as being protected

Elliptic has a word for the thing that actually matters: operationalizing risk. Strip the jargon and it means acting on every single transaction, not writing a policy and filing it in a drawer. Complyadvantage makes the same point from the other end: almost nobody gets burned for lacking an AML policy. They get burned because the policy couldn't reach the payment that was sitting right in front of them.

So forget the paperwork for a second. Two things actually change your odds.

Stop checking wallets by hand. Manual checks are slow, they are inconsistent, and they are the first thing to break the moment you get busy. You miss the one that mattered, your clean customers wait around for no reason, and none of it survives past a handful of payments a day.

Check before the money settles, not after. This is the whole game. After a bad payment lands you are doing damage control, freezes and lawyers and explanations. Before it lands you still have the one thing that matters: a choice.

Bottom line

Criminals don't pick you because you did something wrong. They pick you because, until now, you had no way to see them coming. That is the entire reason, and it is the one thing on this whole page you can actually fix today.
